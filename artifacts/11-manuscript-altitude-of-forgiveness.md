# ALTITUDE OF FORGIVENESS

## Chapter 1 — Airport Rain
By the time the rain found her, Luna Álvarez had already been left once that morning.

Mateo's message sat open on her phone like a legal notice: *I can't do this trip. I can't do us. Don't wait.*

At Lima's airport curb, buses hissed, taxis shouted, and strangers held signs with names belonging to luckier people. Luna had two backpacks, one dead power bank, and exactly eleven percent battery left in the phone she wanted to throw into traffic.

"Luna?"

She knew that voice before she turned. Gabriel stood ten paces away, older than the last version of him she allowed herself to remember, holding a cream-colored urn against his chest with both hands.

"No," she said, because she had no better defense than denial.

He stopped where he was. "Transit strike started an hour ago. It's not safe to move around alone tonight."

"You don't get to brief me."

He nodded once, accepting the blow. "I booked one room near Miraflores. One bed, one chair. One night. Then you disappear and I don't follow."

She hated that he knew how to bargain with damage.

By midnight, she was asleep in the chair while his mother rested in an urn by the window and his daughter breathed ten feet away like a second chance with bad timing.

## Chapter 2 — Two Toothbrushes, One Sink
Morning made everything less dramatic and more humiliating.

Luna woke with neck pain and mascara tracks. Gabriel was already dressed, reading a folded city map as if paper could make life simpler.

"Coffee?" he asked.

"No." She took it anyway.

On the dresser, the urn watched them fail at being civil.

"Whose ashes?" she asked.

"Abuela Sofía. She wanted the Andes."

The word *wanted* stung. Dead women still got what they wanted in this family.

At breakfast, Luna said she was going to Cusco alone.

"There's still strike spillover," Gabriel said. "At least take this." He slid a rail ticket across the table.

"I didn't ask for your help."

"I know."

She stared at the ticket, at his hands, at the scar near his thumb from the kitchen accident when she was nine and still believed fathers were invincible.

She folded the ticket into her passport.

"This isn't forgiveness," she said.

"Good," he replied. "I don't trust cheap versions of it."

## Chapter 3 — Thin Air, Thick Silence
Cusco made breathing feel like a moral test.

By the time they reached the hostel stairs, Luna's lungs were bargaining with gravity. Gabriel pretended not to notice her pauses, and she let him pretend because pride was cheaper than vulnerability.

In the room, she unzipped her backpack searching for ibuprofen and found an envelope tucked between sweaters. Spanish cedar paper. Her mother's handwriting.

No name outside. Inside: both of theirs.

*If you're reading this together, it means the universe got impatient with both of you.*

Luna laughed once, too loud, too close to tears.

Gabriel looked up from the sink. "What happened?"

She handed him the page. His face changed the way weather changes over mountain roads—quickly, without negotiation.

"She wrote more?" he whispered.

"Apparently she planned a scavenger hunt in the afterlife."

The letter ended with instructions: *Find the second envelope where water runs backward.*

"What does that even mean?" Luna asked.

Gabriel stared through the window at roofs and cathedrals and history stacked wrong.

"It means your mother is still impossible," he said.

For the first time in eight years, Luna almost smiled at something he said.

## Chapter 4 — The Envelope in Spanish Cedar
The second envelope waited at a fountain in San Blas where water curled around carved stone and seemed, in evening light, to climb instead of fall.

Tourists took photos. A street musician played a cracked violin. Luna reached into a niche behind the basin and found cedar paper wrapped in wax.

Back in the hostel, they read in shifts.

Sofía's letter was not kind. It was accurate.

Dates. Calls. Voicemails. The night Luna left at twenty, believing Gabriel chose his new partner over his daughter. The letter claimed Sofía had redirected messages, edited details, postponed meetings "until emotions were safer." By the time truth arrived, everyone had preferred their own version.

"She lied," Luna said.

Gabriel swallowed. "She curated."

"No. That's your favorite synonym for cowardice."

Outside, festival drums climbed the walls.

"I should've come after you," he said.

"That's not enough."

He tried again. "I stayed away because I was proud and because I believed you'd return when you were ready. I made your pain about my fear."

Luna nodded once. Not absolution. Not rejection. A hinge moving for the first time.

## Chapter 5 — Rules for Temporary Peace
They wrote rules on hotel stationery:

1. No shouting in public.
2. No rewriting facts mid-argument.
3. If one person says "oxygen," conversation pauses.
4. No using dead people as shields.

"Add one," Luna said. "No calling this healing."

Gabriel wrote it down.

They took a colectivo toward the Sacred Valley, knees touching whenever the van hit potholes. Every accidental contact felt like trespassing.

In Pisac's market, Luna bought a silver ring she didn't need because empty fingers made abandoned things too obvious.

Gabriel bought coca tea and forgot his wallet at the previous stall. They argued in three languages and half-sentences until the vendor laughed and handed the wallet back.

"You fight like married people," the woman said.

"Worse," Luna replied. "We're family."

That night, Luna drafted a message to Mateo and deleted it. Gabriel drafted an apology to Luna and saved it to drafts. Neither pressed send.

## Chapter 6 — Festival Night
Music erased distances faster than apologies.

In Ollantaytambo, a local procession flooded the square with candles and brass horns. Luna watched dancers in embroidered masks circle firelight while Gabriel stood beside her holding two paper cups of chicha morada like offerings.

"Your mother loved this," he said.

"You always say what she loved when you don't know what to say to me."

He winced but didn't retreat. "You're right."

They sat on a curb and let children run between them like punctuation marks.

"Did you love her more than me?" Luna asked quietly.

Gabriel answered too quickly. "No."

"Then why did it feel like I had to compete with ghosts?"

He looked at the candle wax pooling on stone. "Because I was better at grieving privately than loving publicly."

Luna stared at him for a long time, then rested her shoulder against his for exactly five seconds before standing up.

"Don't make that a milestone," she said.

"I won't," he lied.

## Chapter 7 — The Wrong Villain
The third envelope was hidden in a bookshop that sold maps older than modern certainty.

Sofía's letter named a person neither expected: Valeria, Gabriel's former partner. According to Sofía, Valeria had encouraged distance between father and daughter during the worst year, framing Luna as "volatile" and Gabriel as "fragile."

Luna's anger found a target and sprinted.

"So that's it? Evil stepmother cliché?"

"No," Gabriel said. "I chose convenience. She didn't force me."

"You keep saying brave things after the damage is antique."

He flinched. "Fair."

"I don't want fair. I want impossible: I want sixteen-year-old me to not wait by the window."

The room held that sentence like a held breath.

Gabriel sat down on the floor because standing felt dishonest. "Tell me about that night," he said.

Luna did. For the first time, he did not defend, contextualize, or redirect.

When she finished, both of them looked exhausted in a way sleep couldn't fix.

## Chapter 8 — Market of Echoes
In Urubamba's Sunday market, a journalist recognized Gabriel from an old municipal scandal back home and asked for a quote about "family values and personal accountability." The irony was so violent Luna laughed.

"No comment," Gabriel said.

The journalist followed anyway, filming on a phone. Luna stepped between them.

"Back off," she snapped.

"Public figure," the woman said.

"Grieving son," Luna replied, pointing to the urn strapped inside Gabriel's pack.

The clip still went online before sunset: father and daughter in a crowded market, mouths moving, captions inventing context.

By dinner, Mateo had texted Luna a link and a single line: *Wow. You're still chaos.*

She threw the phone onto the bed.

"What happened?" Gabriel asked.

"Nothing new," she said. "Men I trusted narrating me incorrectly."

He sat across from her, hands open. "Do you want silence or company?"

She surprised herself. "Company. Quiet company."

They ate soup in near darkness while rain struck the tin roof like impatient fingers.

## Chapter 9 — Burial Altitude
At the pass above Ausangate, wind erased easy language.

Gabriel carried the urn with ceremonial care. Luna carried the map and a resentment she could no longer sort cleanly from love.

When they reached the overlook Sofía had marked years earlier, Gabriel opened the urn and found, tucked beneath the ashes, a final folded note.

*Do not scatter me until you tell each other one unedited truth.*

Luna laughed bitterly. "She even micromanages as dust."

"She's consistent," Gabriel said.

"Fine. Unedited truth?" Luna's jaw tightened. "I learned to be unlovable first so nobody could fire me from their life again."

The wind stole her breath after that confession. Gabriel stepped closer but did not touch her.

"My unedited truth," he said. "I sometimes preferred being admired to being needed. It made me a safer man in public and a worse father in private."

They stood with those truths between them until Luna nodded.

They scattered Sofía into mountain light.

Ash and snow braided in the same air.

## Chapter 10 — Switchback
Descent should have been easier. It wasn't.

At a narrow turn, Luna stopped, knees shaking, vision tunneling. Altitude panic arrived like a stolen staircase.

"I can't—" she whispered.

"Oxygen," Gabriel said, invoking Rule Three.

Conversation ceased. He wrapped the emergency blanket around her shoulders, counted breaths with her, matched pace. No philosophy. No confession. Just numbers.

"Look at me," he said. "Four in, six out. Again."

She obeyed because fear outranked pride.

After twenty minutes, color returned to the world.

"Don't make this symbolic," she said hoarsely.

"I'm not. You had a physiological response."

She laughed once. "You sound like a nurse in a court deposition."

"Occupational hazard of guilt," he said.

For the first time, her smile stayed.

## Chapter 11 — White Noise Snow
Storm trapped them at a mountain lodge with six strangers, one wood stove, and no cell signal.

Someone started a card game. Someone else cried quietly in the pantry over a breakup in Portuguese. Human drama leveled social classes fast.

Luna and Gabriel shared a blanket by necessity. He smelled like cedar soap and cold air.

"I told Mateo things about you," Luna admitted. "Cruel things. Some true, some performative."

"I'm not applying for sainthood," Gabriel said.

"I know. Still." She swallowed. "I weaponized you to avoid grieving mom. If I made you the villain, I didn't have to face how much I missed both of you."

His eyes filled, then steadied.

"Thank you," he said simply.

No embrace. No cinematic swell. Just two people choosing precision over theater while snow erased the road outside.

## Chapter 12 — Campfire Accounting
When roads reopened, they camped one final night before descending to Puerto Maldonado.

Gabriel built a small fire. Luna opened the notebook where they'd begun listing facts versus stories.

**Fact:** Dad missed the recital at fourteen.
**Story:** He didn't care.
**New data:** He was in ER with Abuela, who told him not to call and "ruin your exam week."

**Fact:** Luna stopped answering for eight years.
**Story:** She hated him.
**New data:** She feared being persuaded out of her anger before she could understand it.

They added lines until midnight.

"This is exhausting," Luna said.

"Truth usually is. Lies are efficient."

"Are we fixed?"

Gabriel poked the fire. "No. But we're no longer fictional."

That was better.

## Chapter 13 — River Boat Morning
The Amazon light felt indecently alive after mountain austerity.

On a slow river boat, Luna read the last envelope while Gabriel watched pink dolphins surface like punctuation.

Sofía's closing note was brief:

*Love without accuracy becomes control. Accuracy without love becomes punishment. Learn the middle before I did.*

Luna passed the paper to Gabriel.

"She always wrote like a judge trying to be a poet," he said.

"And yet she's right. Annoying even dead."

They laughed at the same time, startled by harmony.

"When we go home," Luna said, "I don't want a dramatic reset."

"Agreed."

"Monthly recorded calls. No disappearing. No triangulating through other people."

"Done."

"If one of us lies by omission, we restart from zero."

He extended his hand.

She shook it.

A contract, not a miracle.

## Chapter 14 — Return Ticket
Lima again. Departure boards. The same rain, less hostile.

At security, Luna's phone buzzed with a message from Mateo: *Can we talk?*

She typed: *No. I hope you heal well. Goodbye.*

She did not tremble after sending it.

Gabriel's phone lit with Valeria's name. He stared, then muted it.

"No callbacks to old architecture," he said.

"Good policy."

They stood in line, two people no longer pretending indifference was strength.

At the gate, Gabriel cleared his throat. "I'm proud of the woman you became without my help. I'm sorry you had to."

Luna's eyes shone. "I'm proud of you for finally saying things while they still matter."

Final boarding echoed overhead.

They hugged once, briefly, like people handling a rare object correctly.

When they separated, neither called it closure.

They called it beginning.
